**Abstract** *(original abstract, verbatim from PubMed)*

> BACKGROUND: Current guidelines recommend routine hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance in patients with cirrhosis when annual HCC incidence exceeds 1 per 100 person-years, a threshold derived from older studies and not specific to alcohol-associated cirrhosis. However, contemporary data suggest that HCC incidence in this population is often below 1%, leaving the value of routine surveillance uncertain. METHODS: We developed a Markov-based state-transition microsimulation model to simulate the natural history of HCC and subsequent treatment pathways in patients with alcohol-associated cirrhosis in the United States setting. The model compared biannual surveillance with ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) versus no surveillance separately for two distinct cohorts: patients who continued to actively consume alcohol and patients who had achieved sustained abstinence. Inputs included the natural history of alcohol-associated cirrhosis, HCC incidence, test performance, surveillance adherence, and contemporary treatment. Sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of model assumptions and findings. RESULTS: Biannual surveillance becomes cost-effective when annual HCC incidence reached approximately 0.45 per 100 person-years using willingness-to-pay threshold of \$100,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). Among active drinkers, surveillance yielded an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of \$18,952 per QALY; among abstinent patients, the ICER was \$56,000 per QALY. Per 100,000 patients, routine surveillance generated an estimated 2,083 additional life-years and 992 QALYs in active drinkers and 3,357 life-years and 1,700 QALYs in abstinent patients. CONCLUSION: Routine biannual HCC surveillance with ultrasound and AFP is cost-effective for patients with alcohol-associated cirrhosis in the United States when HCC incidence exceeds 0.45 per 100 person-years, substantially below the historically cited 1 per 100 person-years threshold. Updating clinical guidelines to reflect these contemporary thresholds could improve early HCC detection in this high-risk population.

**Abstract** *(original abstract, verbatim from PubMed)*

> OBJECTIVE: This study aimed to evaluate the conventional and distributional cost-effectiveness of semaglutide, laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), and a "do nothing" option in Black and White racial subgroups. METHODS: A Markov simulation model was developed from a healthcare perspective over a 25-year time horizon for Black and White adults, aged 65-70, with BMI ≥ 35 and cardiovascular disease. Three interventions were modeled: semaglutide 2.4 mg, LSG, and "do nothing." RESULTS: Semaglutide and LSG interventions are cost-effective compared to a "do nothing" strategy (ICERs = \$7300/QALY and \$12,100/QALY, respectively). Compared to semaglutide, LSG is more expensive but cost-effective (ICER = \$53,700/QALY). Semaglutide is more equitable but is not distributionally cost-effective compared to LSG, because it has a lower EDE within our defined inequality aversion (ε) range. Across 10,000 probabilistic iterations, LSG has a 57% probability and semaglutide has a 43% probability of being the most cost-effective at the willingness-to-pay threshold of \$100,000/QALY. Semaglutide becomes distributionally cost-effective when ε ≥ 14.72, where equity impacts of semaglutide outweigh the conventional cost-effectiveness outcomes. CONCLUSIONS: LSG is the most conventionally cost-effective treatment option with some uncertainty. Semaglutide is marginally more equitable than LSG but is not a distributionally cost-effective strategy.

**Abstract** *(original abstract, verbatim from PubMed)*

> AIMS: The National Health Service (NHS) offers Healthy Living, an online Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support service, to help people Self Manage Type 2 Diabetes, with modest 1-year reductions in HbA1c levels, systolic blood pressure and body mass index. We aim to assess the cost-effectiveness of Healthy Living compared with usual care. METHODS: A patient-level economic simulation model (UKPDS-OM2.1) was used to conduct an economic analysis from the NHS perspective over a lifetime horizon, discounted at 3.5%. The model translated risk factor reductions to long-term cost and quality-adjusted life year (QALY) estimates. Additional analyses estimated intervention and medication cost consequences. RESULTS: The UKPDS-OM2.1 predicted a modest improvement in health (0.016 QALYs) and a small reduction in lifetime healthcare costs (-£4 per person). Offsetting with intervention costs (£7 per user) and increased medication costs (£212 per user) yielded a net cost of £216 per user. The cost-per-QALY of £13,192 was below the lower NICE threshold (£25,000/QALY), indicating that the programme is cost-effective. Sensitivity analyses largely demonstrated robustness, but the finding was sensitive to structural assumptions about disease progression post-intervention with the inclusion of medication costs. CONCLUSION: Healthy Living appears to be a low-cost, online intervention for self managing type 2 diabetes, generating modest health benefits that are likely to be considered cost-effective use of NHS resources. While there is some uncertainty in the cost-effectiveness and the modelling is based on effectiveness estimated from observational data, improvements in uptake and adherence has the potential to improve the value for money to the NHS.
